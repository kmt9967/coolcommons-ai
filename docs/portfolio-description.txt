CoolCommons AI — Turning climate data into cooler, safer communities
Team Teqprotech (Team ID E10): Khawaja Muhammad Talal, Aqeela Urooj, Umer Anis, Aliya Yousuf

Karachi's summers are getting harder to live through. Humid air from the Arabian Sea makes the heat people feel far worse than the thermometer suggests. At midday on 16 June 2025, the apparent ("feels-like") temperature for Karachi reached 43.7 °C while the air temperature was 38 °C. Days with an apparent temperature of 39 °C or more rose to 41 in 2024 and 39 in 2025, compared with 4 to 16 a year between 2016 and 2023.

City teams, NGOs and community groups know heat is dangerous, but they have limited budgets and little time. They need to know where drinking water, shade, a cool indoor space or simple heat information would help the most people. Climate data rarely arrives in a form that answers that question.

CoolCommons AI is a free web app that helps them plan. It starts with real climate records for Karachi and keeps today's forecast separate from history. A community map shows nearly 2,000 libraries, community centres, parks, clinics and water points from OpenStreetMap. Each one is presented as a possible site to assess, not as a confirmed cooling centre. In the simulator, a planner can add hypothetical water points, shaded rest areas or cooling spaces, or let an optimisation algorithm suggest where new sites would reach the most unserved area, and then compare the area within reach before and after. A short exposure check combines a real hot day with local conditions, such as hours outdoors, physical activity and shade, and every part of the score is labelled as data, an assumption or a scenario. Finally, an AI planner powered by Google Gemini turns this evidence into a practical, low-cost action plan that covers accessibility, resources, priorities and the assumptions behind it.

The aim is better-targeted heat adaptation: helping local organisations put limited resources where they extend access most, using evidence they can see and question. CoolCommons AI is an early prototype. It has not yet been tested with users in the field, and it makes no claims about temperature reduction, people reached or health outcomes. Our next step is to co-design and test it with a Karachi NGO or municipal team before the next hot season, adding population and land-surface temperature data.

— TECHNICAL DETAILS & DISCLOSURES —
Data: Open-Meteo Historical Weather API (ERA5/ERA5-Land reanalysis, modelled, about 9–25 km grid cell centred 24.85°N 66.99°E, CC BY 4.0) and Open-Meteo Forecast API. "Apparent temperature" is Open-Meteo's feels-like measure combining air temperature, humidity and wind, not the US NWS heat index; NWS heat-index bands (27/32/39/51 °C) are used only as an indicative screening scale. The 43.7 °C value is the hourly peak at 12:00 PKT (relative humidity 42% at that hour; daily mean 74%). Facilities: 1,950 OpenStreetMap features in central Karachi (ODbL), retrieved 16 Nov 2026. Basemap © Esri.
AI: Google Gemini Flash models via the free-tier Gemini API (gemini-2.5-flash, falling back to Flash-Lite models when overloaded), called server-side only. Gemini receives only the structured context shown in the app. Safeguards include input validation, rate limits, timeouts, token limits, JSON-schema validation, caching and quota handling. If Gemini is unavailable, a rules-based engine produces the plan and is clearly labelled "no AI model called". Live Gemini output on the production site was verified on 16 Nov 2026.
Limitations: city-scale reanalysis cannot show street-level microclimates. OSM data may be incomplete. The access measure is area-based and straight-line, not population coverage. The exposure-score weights have not been validated against health outcomes. AI output must be reviewed by people with local knowledge. 12 automated unit tests pass.
Prior work and AI assistance: no code was reused from earlier team projects. Built with Next.js, React, Tailwind CSS, Recharts, Leaflet and zod. Code, documentation, slides and the demo were produced with substantial help from an AI coding assistant (Claude Code), directed and reviewed by the team. The demo narration uses a synthetic voice (Microsoft Andrew Neural).
Timing: the CoolCommons AI code and materials were created on 16 November 2026 (see git history), after the official 14–15 November core-development window.
Licence: code MIT; data keeps its original licences.
